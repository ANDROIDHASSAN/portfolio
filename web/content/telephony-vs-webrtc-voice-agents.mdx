Browser demos lie to you, a little.

In a browser your agent gets a clean, wideband microphone with echo cancellation built in, over a low-latency WebRTC connection. Then you put the same agent on a phone number, and it starts mishearing names, talking over people and getting voicemail greetings as answers.

I built IncScreen, an AI phone-screening product, from scratch on Twilio and Exotel telephony with Vapi orchestrating the speech pipeline. This is the list I wish I'd had on day one.

## 1. The audio is a different medium

| | Browser (WebRTC) | Phone line (PSTN) |
|---|---|---|
| Codec | Opus | G.711 (μ-law or A-law) |
| Sample rate | up to 48 kHz | 8 kHz |
| Frequencies carried | full voice range | roughly 300–3,400 Hz |
| Echo cancellation | built into the browser | whatever the handset and carrier do |

That 8 kHz limit throws away the high frequencies where a lot of consonants live: the difference between "s" and "f", or between similar-sounding names. Phone media streams from providers like Twilio arrive as 8 kHz μ-law audio, and that's all the information there is.

What that means in practice:

- **Use STT that's good on telephony audio.** Upsampling 8 kHz to 16 kHz doesn't recover what the line removed. Pick models and settings meant for phone calls, and test with real phone recordings, not your laptop mic.
- **Keyterm prompting matters more.** With less acoustic detail, the model leans harder on its vocabulary guesses. Tell it the names it's about to hear ([how](/blog/keyterm-prompting-voice-agents)).
- **Your expensive TTS voice gets squeezed.** That wideband, expressive voice is downsampled to 8 kHz on the way out. Listen to every candidate voice *through a phone* before you pick one. Some survive narrowband much better than others.

## 2. Nobody is cancelling the echo for you

In a browser, `echoCancellation: true` handles the agent hearing itself. On a phone call, the caller might be on speakerphone in a noisy room, and the agent's own voice comes back down the line. Without care, the agent interrupts *itself*.

So everything in [the barge-in post](/blog/false-interruptions-barge-in) gets more important on phones: noise and background-voice cancellation on the incoming audio, a little evidence before treating sound as an interruption, and recovery when it was a false alarm. IncScreen's Hindi calls had exactly this class of problem: cut-off speech and interruptions.

## 3. Latency has more hops

A browser call goes caller → WebRTC server → your agent. A phone call goes caller → carrier → telephony provider → your agent, and back. Each leg adds delay and jitter buffering you don't control.

You can't remove those hops, so you budget for them. If the network eats more of the budget, the pipeline has to give some back: stream everything, start TTS on the first sentence, generate the reply before the turn is fully final. On IncScreen, Hindi replies took about 5 seconds against about 2 in English, and on a phone call 5 seconds of silence feels like the line dropped. That's why every stage gets a number in [the latency budget](/blog/sub-second-hindi-voice-agent).

## 4. The phone network has its own vocabulary

Things a browser agent never deals with:

- **Voicemail and answering machines.** On outbound calls, plenty of "answers" are a recorded greeting. Without detection, your agent politely interviews a voicemail box. IncScreen has voicemail detection for exactly this reason.
- **Call states.** Ringing, answered, busy, no answer, failed, completed. Providers report them through webhooks, and your system has to handle every one, including the boring ones, because "no answer" is a business event (retry? notify someone?).
- **Keypad tones (DTMF).** The universal fallback input. When speech recognition struggles, "press 1 for yes" still works on every phone ever made.
- **Transfers.** Sometimes the right move is to hand the caller to a human. That's a telephony action, not an LLM response.

## 5. Picking a provider and a level of control

**Twilio** is the global default: mature APIs, media streams over WebSockets, numbers in many countries. **Exotel** is built around Indian telephony: Indian numbers and the way business calling works in India, which has its own telecom regulations. For an India-first product it's worth having both in the toolbox.

Then there's how much of the pipeline you own:

| Approach | You get | You give up |
|---|---|---|
| Orchestration platform (e.g. Vapi) | STT, LLM, TTS and telephony wired together; fast to ship | Fine control over turn-taking, routing and failure handling |
| Your own agent on raw telephony (e.g. LiveKit SIP, media streams) | Every knob: per-language routing, custom fallbacks, your own guards | You build and run all of it |

Neither is "the senior choice". Shipping a working phone product fast on a platform, then pulling pieces in-house where the platform can't do what you need, is a perfectly good path. The senior part is knowing which pieces those are.

<Callout>
Before you launch a phone agent, call it from a cheap phone, on speakerphone, in a noisy room, on a weak signal. Then let it call a number that goes to voicemail. Most of the bugs in this post show up in those two calls.
</Callout>

## The short version

Treat the phone line as a separate platform, not a deployment target. Different audio, different echo, different latency, different events. An agent that's great in the browser is a strong starting point, not a finished phone product.
